=== IN SHORT ===
IELTS Writing Task 1 Academic asks for a report of at least 150 words on a chart, table, map or process, written in about 20 minutes.
The overview, a short summary of the main trends or differences, is what separates a Band 6 or 7 report from a Band 5 one.
A good Task 1 report selects the key features and supports them with figures, instead of describing every number.
Academic Task 1 is a factual report, so opinions and guesses about causes earn no credit.

=== BODY ===
In IELTS Writing Task 1 Academic you have about 20 minutes and at least 150 words to describe a visual: a line graph, a bar chart, a pie chart, a table, a map or a process diagram. Most candidates know the vocabulary of rising and falling. What costs them marks is structure, and in particular one paragraph that many reports leave out.

## What IELTS Writing Task 1 is really testing

Task 1 is marked on the same four criteria as Task 2, with one name changed: **Task Achievement** instead of Task Response. Task Achievement asks whether you reported the most important features of the visual, accurately, with an overview. The public band descriptors are explicit about the overview. A clear overview of the main trends, differences or stages is part of the Band 7 description, while a report that recounts details mechanically with no clear overview is described at Band 5.

## A four-paragraph plan

1. **Introduction, one sentence.** Say what the visual shows, in your own words. Change the wording of the question, not its meaning.
2. **Overview, two sentences.** The two or three things a reader should notice first: the biggest change, the highest and the lowest, the general direction. No figures are needed here.
3. **First body paragraph.** The first group of details, with figures and comparisons.
4. **Second body paragraph.** The second group of details, with figures and comparisons.

Four paragraphs of this shape usually come to 160 to 190 words, which is enough. A Task 1 report has no conclusion in the essay sense, and it never gives an opinion. If you prefer, the overview can come last, but it has to be there.

## How to write the overview

An overview describes the whole picture without the details. Ask yourself two questions: what is the general trend, and what stands out?

:::example A line graph of visitors to three museums, 2014 to 2024
Detail: The Science Museum had 420,000 visitors in 2014 and 545,000 in 2024.
Overview: Overall, the Science Museum was the most visited of the three throughout the period, and all three saw a sharp fall in 2020, after which the Science and Maritime museums rose above their earlier levels.
:::

The first sentence is true, but it is a detail. The second tells the reader what matters before a single number appears, and it is the sentence an examiner looks for.

## Choosing and grouping the details

You cannot describe every figure in 150 words, and you are not supposed to. Group the data by what it has in common: the categories that rose and the ones that fell, the largest and the smallest, the period before a change and the period after it. Then give one or two figures for each group.

- Give figures with their units and dates: *rose from 150,000 in 2014 to 305,000 in 2024*.
- Compare, do not just list: *more than twice as many*, *slightly lower than*, *the largest share*.
- Use the tense of the data: the past for past years, and *is expected to* for projections.

Accuracy matters more than variety. A figure misread from the chart is a Task Achievement problem, however elegant the sentence around it.

## The language of change and comparison

:::example Trends
Up: rose, increased, grew, climbed
Down: fell, decreased, declined, dropped
Highest point: peaked at, reached a high of
Little change: remained stable, levelled off, stayed at around
Up and down: fluctuated between
:::

:::example Comparisons
Ratio: twice as many as, half as much as
Small gap: slightly higher than, marginally lower than
Big gap: considerably more than, far fewer than
Rank: the largest share, the second most popular
:::

Mix verb and noun forms, such as *visitor numbers rose sharply* and *there was a sharp rise in visitor numbers*, but only where both sound natural. A sentence that strains for variety is harder to read than a plain one.

## Five mistakes that keep Task 1 at Band 5

1. **No overview**, or a single vague sentence at the end that summarises nothing.
2. **Every number described** in the order it appears, with no grouping and no comparison.
3. **Opinions and causes**, such as *this happened because people prefer museums*. The chart does not say why, so neither should you.
4. **The question copied** word for word in the introduction. Words copied from the question are not counted, and they show nothing about your English.
5. **Fewer than 150 words.** A short report cannot cover enough of the visual to score well for Task Achievement.

:::tip Spend two minutes looking before you write
Before your first sentence, find the overview: the biggest change and the one feature that stands out. Once you know them, the report almost writes itself, and you will not run out of time with the most important sentence still missing.
:::

=== FAQ ===
Q: How many words should IELTS Writing Task 1 be?
A: At least 150 words, written in about 20 minutes. Most strong reports are between 160 and 190 words; writing much more uses minutes that Task 2, which counts twice as much, needs.

Q: Do I need a conclusion in IELTS Writing Task 1?
A: You need an overview, not a conclusion. The overview summarises the main trends or differences in one or two sentences, and it can come after the introduction or at the end.

Q: Should I describe every number in a Task 1 chart?
A: No. Select the key features, group them, and support each group with one or two figures. Describing every number usually produces a report with no overview.

Q: Can I give my opinion in IELTS Academic Task 1?
A: No. Academic Task 1 is a factual report on the visual. Opinions and guesses about why something happened are not in the data, and they earn no credit.
