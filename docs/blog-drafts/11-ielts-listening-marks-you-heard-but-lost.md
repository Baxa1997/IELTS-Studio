=== IN SHORT ===
Most marks lost in IELTS Listening are answers the candidate heard correctly and then wrote down wrongly.
A misspelt word, a missing plural or an answer over the word limit is marked wrong, even when the meaning is right.
Speakers in the recording often give one detail and then correct it, and the corrected detail is the answer.
Reading the questions before each part begins tells you what kind of word to listen for.
Checking spelling, plurals and the word limit at the end is the fastest way to add marks without hearing anything new.

=== BODY ===
Ask candidates why they lost marks in Listening and most will say the recording was too fast. Look at their answer sheets and a different story appears. The answer was heard. It was then written with one letter wrong, without its final *s*, or with one word too many. In IELTS Listening each of the 40 questions is worth one mark, and there are no half marks for nearly right.

That is good news. Understanding faster English takes months. Writing down what you already heard, correctly, takes a few careful habits.

## The test in one paragraph

IELTS Listening has four parts and 40 questions, and lasts about 30 minutes. You hear each recording **once**. Part 1 is an everyday conversation, Part 2 an everyday talk by one speaker, Part 3 a discussion in a study setting, and Part 4 a short lecture. The questions follow the order of the recording, so the answer to question 5 always comes before the answer to question 6.

## Mark one: spelling

A misspelt answer is a wrong answer. The words that cost the most marks are ordinary ones:

:::example Words candidates often misspell
Days and months: Wednesday, February
Double letters: accommodation, address, different
Silent letters: government, environment, library
Easily swapped: quiet and quite, weather and whether
:::

Both British and American spellings are accepted, so *colour* and *color* are both right. Choose one and keep to it.

When a name or a postcode is spelt out letter by letter, write each letter as you hear it and do not try to guess the word. Names in Part 1 are often chosen because they could be spelt two ways.

## Mark two: the final s

English marks the plural with one small sound, and it is easy to miss when you are writing quickly. If the sentence on the page reads *The centre offers free ______ for members* and the speaker says *lockers*, then *locker* is wrong.

The sentence around the gap usually tells you which form you need. *A* or *an* before the gap means a singular noun. *Many*, *several* or a number means a plural. Read the sentence again with your answer in it. If it sounds wrong, it probably is.

## Mark three: the word limit

Every set of questions carries an instruction such as *Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER*. An answer of three words is marked wrong, even if it contains the right two.

:::tip Read the instruction every time
The limit changes from one set of questions to the next within the same test. Underline it, or note it, before the recording starts. A correct answer that breaks the limit earns nothing.
:::

You never need to change the words you hear. The answer is in the recording in the form the gap needs, so if your answer only fits after you rewrite it, look again.

## Mark four: the detail that changes

The recording often gives you an answer and then takes it away.

> "So the workshop is on the fourteenth. Oh, sorry, that week is full. It has been moved to the sixteenth."

A candidate who writes at the first number writes *14* and stops listening. The answer is *16*. Speakers correct themselves, change their minds and compare options, and the questions are written to test whether you followed all of it.

The habit that helps is simple: when you hear a possible answer, note it lightly and keep listening until the speaker moves on to something else.

## Use the time before each part

Before each part you are given a short time to look at the questions. Use all of it, and decide what **kind** of word each gap needs: a number, a place, a day, a name, a thing. When you know you are waiting for a price, you hear the price.

Look also at the two or three words on each side of the gap. The recording will not use them exactly. It will say the same thing in other words, and those words are your signal that the answer is coming.

## Check before you finish

At the end of the test you have a short time to review your answers. Do not spend it worrying about questions you missed. Go through what you wrote and check three things for every answer:

- **Spelling:** is every word spelt correctly?
- **Form:** singular or plural, and does it fit the sentence?
- **Limit:** is it within the number of words allowed?

An empty gap earns nothing and a wrong guess loses nothing, so write an answer for every question.

## Practise the way you will be marked

When you practise, mark yourself the way the test does. A nearly right answer is a wrong one, and it should count as wrong in your own score too, or your practice band will be higher than your real one. Then look at **why** each mark was lost. If most of them are spelling and plurals, you do not have a listening problem. You have a checking problem, and that is the quickest kind to fix.

=== FAQ ===
Q: Does spelling matter in IELTS Listening?
A: Yes. A misspelt answer is marked wrong, even when it is clear which word you meant. Both British and American spellings are accepted.

Q: What happens if I write more words than the limit allows?
A: The answer is marked wrong. If the instruction says no more than two words, a three-word answer earns no mark, even if it includes the correct words.

Q: How many times do you hear the recording in IELTS Listening?
A: Once. Each of the four parts is played a single time, and the questions follow the order of the recording.

Q: Do I lose marks for a wrong answer in IELTS Listening?
A: No. A wrong answer and an empty answer both score zero, so it is always worth writing an answer for every question.

Q: How many correct answers do I need for Band 7 in Listening?
A: About 30 out of 40. The exact number changes slightly between versions of the test.
