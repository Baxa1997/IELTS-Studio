=== IN SHORT ===
The CEFR Multilevel exam is Uzbekistan's national English exam, run by the State Testing Centre (DTM).
The Multilevel exam reports a CEFR level such as B1, B2 or C1 instead of an IELTS band from 0 to 9.
The Multilevel Reading paper has five parts and 35 questions, and each part is harder than the one before it.
The Multilevel Writing paper asks for the same situation twice, once informally and once formally.

=== BODY ===
Many candidates in Uzbekistan who sit the CEFR Multilevel exam prepare with IELTS books because that is what they can find. That is a mistake. The two exams test the same language but reward different things. Here are seven facts that change how you should prepare.

## 1. CEFR is a scale, not a test

CEFR stands for the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages. It is not an exam at all: it is a scale of six levels, from A1 for a beginner to C2 for near-mastery, and each level is described by what a learner **can do** with the language. The Multilevel exam is one of many tests that report results on that scale.

## 2. One exam, three possible levels

Most language exams are set at one level, and you either pass or fail it. The Multilevel exam is different: in a single sitting it places you at **B1, B2 or C1**. The paper itself climbs through those levels, so the same exam works for a school leaver and for a university graduate.

## 3. Reading gets harder as you go

The Reading paper has five parts, 35 questions and 60 minutes. Part 1 is written at B1 and Part 5 at C1, with every question worth one mark. That shapes your time: the early parts should go quickly, so that you reach the academic text in Part 5 with enough minutes left to read it properly.

:::tip Do not spend the C1 minutes on B1 questions
If a question in Part 1 or Part 2 is taking too long, mark your best guess and move on. A mark in Part 5 is worth exactly the same, and it needs more time to earn.
:::

## 4. In Part 1, the answer is already in the text

In the first Reading part, every gap takes exactly one word — and **the missing word appears somewhere else in the same text**. You are not inventing the answer; you are finding it, then checking that its form fits the gap. No IELTS task works this way.

## 5. Some options are there only to catch you

In Reading Part 2 you match eight short texts to ten statements, so **two statements match nothing**. Part 3 gives you more headings than paragraphs. Part 4 has **No Information**, which works like Not Given in IELTS. In every case, the extra options are built to look right. Read [True, False or Not Given? The question the passage never answers](/blog/true-false-not-given) for how that trap works.

## 6. Writing tests who you are writing to

Writing Task 1 gives you **one situation** and asks you to write about it twice: a short informal email to a friend, then a formal email of 120 to 150 words to someone in charge. The facts barely change. What is marked is **register** — the vocabulary, grammar and tone that suit each reader. Task 2 is a forum post of 180 to 200 words, written at C1.

:::example The same complaint, two readers
To a friend: Have you seen the new prices at the club? I was really annoyed.
To the manager: I am writing to express my concern about the recent increase in membership fees.
:::

## 7. A 6.5 is not a B2

People love converting between IELTS bands and CEFR levels, but the two exams are different instruments with different papers, and a rule of thumb can leave you surprised on results day. Practise the format you will actually sit, and judge yourself by a CEFR level, not a band. For the full part-by-part guide, read [The Multilevel exam explained](/blog/multilevel-exam-explained).
