=== IN SHORT ===
Matching headings asks for the main idea of each paragraph, and a heading that matches only one detail of the paragraph is wrong.
The list always holds more headings than there are paragraphs, so some headings are never used.
Reading the paragraph first and summing it up in your own words works better than reading the headings first.
A heading that repeats a word from the paragraph is often a trap, because correct headings usually say the idea in other words.
Leaving the hardest paragraph until the end makes it easier, because fewer headings remain to choose from.

=== BODY ===
Matching headings looks like the easiest task in IELTS Reading. There is a list of headings and a passage of paragraphs, and you choose a heading for each paragraph. It is also the task where good readers lose the most marks, and nearly always for the same reason: they match **words** when the question is asking them to match **ideas**.

## What the task is really asking

A heading is a summary. The right heading for a paragraph is the one that covers what the **whole** paragraph is about. A heading that describes one sentence of it, however accurately, is wrong.

Three facts about the task shape everything else:

- There are always **more headings than paragraphs**. Some are never used.
- Each heading can be used **once** only.
- The extra headings are not random. They are written to fit a detail in one of the paragraphs.

That last point is the heart of it. For most paragraphs there is a heading that fits the paragraph's main idea, and another that fits one eye-catching detail inside it. The task is choosing between those two.

## Why the obvious method fails

The obvious method is to read the list of headings first, then scan each paragraph for words that match. It feels fast, and it walks straight into the trap. Here is a short paragraph of the kind a passage might contain:

> For most of the twentieth century, city planners treated rivers as a problem to be hidden. Streams were buried in pipes, banks were covered in concrete, and roads were built on top. In the last thirty years the same cities have begun to spend large sums uncovering them again. Seoul removed a motorway to bring back a stream through its centre, and summer temperatures along it fell by several degrees.

Now two headings:

:::example Which heading fits?
Heading i: The cooling effect of water in city centres
Heading ii: A change in how cities treat their rivers
:::

Heading i is true. The paragraph does say that temperatures fell. But that is one detail, in the last sentence, of one example. The paragraph as a whole describes rivers being hidden and then uncovered: a change of attitude. The answer is heading ii, and notice that it shares almost no words with the paragraph.

## A method that works

### 1. Read the paragraph before the headings

Start with the passage, not the list. Read the first paragraph through at normal speed. Do not look at the headings yet. If you read them first, your mind goes hunting for their words.

### 2. Sum it up in a few words of your own

Before you look at the list, say to yourself what the paragraph was about, in four or five words. For the example above: *rivers hidden, now uncovered*. It does not need to be elegant. It needs to be yours.

### 3. Now find the heading closest to your summary

Go to the list and look for the heading that means what you just said. You are now matching an idea to an idea. A heading that merely repeats a word from the paragraph will no longer attract you, because it is not what you wrote.

### 4. Check it against the whole paragraph

Ask one question: does this heading cover the beginning, the middle and the end of the paragraph, or only one part of it? If it only fits one sentence, it is the trap.

### 5. Leave the hard one and come back

If two headings both seem possible, write both numbers beside the paragraph and move on. Every heading you use elsewhere is one fewer to choose from, and the difficult paragraph is often simple by the end.

:::tip The first sentence helps, but it is not the answer
Many paragraphs do state their main idea in the first sentence, so it is a good place to look. But some open with the old view and then argue against it, and their real subject arrives in the middle. Read the whole paragraph before you decide.
:::

## Signs you have chosen the trap

- Your heading shares an unusual word with the paragraph, such as a name, a number or a technical term.
- You can point to the exact sentence that proves it, and to no other.
- You chose it before you had finished reading the paragraph.

A correct heading is usually harder to point to. It is true of the paragraph as a whole, and it tends to use more general words than the paragraph does.

## How much time to give it

Matching headings takes longer than most Reading tasks, because it needs every paragraph read properly. That reading is not wasted. By the time you finish, you know where everything is in the passage, and the questions that follow go faster for it. Keep to about 20 minutes for the whole passage, as you would for any other.

=== FAQ ===
Q: Should I read the headings or the passage first?
A: Read the paragraph first. Sum it up in a few words of your own, then look for the heading closest to your summary. Reading the headings first makes you search for their words and choose headings that match a detail.

Q: Are there extra headings in matching headings?
A: Yes. The list always has more headings than there are paragraphs, each heading is used once at most, and the extra ones are usually written to match a detail in one of the paragraphs.

Q: Is the main idea always in the first sentence?
A: Often, but not always. Some paragraphs begin with a view they then argue against, so the main idea appears in the middle or at the end. Read the whole paragraph before choosing.

Q: Why is my matching headings answer wrong when the heading is true?
A: Because a heading must describe the whole paragraph. A heading can be true of one sentence and still be wrong, if the rest of the paragraph is about something else.

Q: How long should matching headings take?
A: Keep to about 20 minutes for the whole passage, including its other questions. The careful reading the task needs also makes the questions after it faster.
